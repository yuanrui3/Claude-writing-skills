# Real people into characters

The author sometimes gives real people as prototypes. The aim: each character reads as a real person, and nobody who knows the original can pick them out. A first pass that changes only the labels is usually still too easy to see through. Start at the stricter level.

## 1. Take the brief
For each person, a few lines from the author is enough:
- what they're like: habits, a line they'd say, what they want, where they fall short;
- what must survive into the character, and what's off-limits;
- a host character, if the author has one in mind;
- a photo, optional (then use the photo-to-waimao skill).

If something is missing, choose and say what you chose; the author doesn't micromanage. Ask only when a person is a public figure, or overlaps with prototypes a book has ruled out.

## 2. Sort the brief into three piles
- **Keep:** behaviour, voice, wants, weaknesses, the emotional spine of a relationship.
- **Change:** job, field, nationality, age, place, family facts, dates, distinctive possessions, signature looks, and any event a third party could recognise.
- **Move:** a striking real detail that identifies the person where it is goes onto a different character, ideally one with no link to the original.

Write the piles only in a scratch file (see section 6).

## 3. Choose hosts
- Existing characters first. Use the book's Map to find thin or unsettled ones, and match by fit, not by role.
- A rich person can be split across two characters. Someone who would only be a minor figure is folded into an existing character, never added under a new name.
- Two real people who know each other shouldn't land on two characters who know each other in the same way.

## 4. Write
- Nobody is evil. Each acts as they're built, with their own interests. A kind original stays kind and is not written cynically (an outside reader may still read them as mean; that read is input only).
- For a relationship that matters (a teacher and student who fall out, say), invent the events and keep only the feeling and its turn.
- Appearance: physical facts plus one cutting detail, through a point-of-view character's eyes. Shift the signature details of a recognisable look (a jacket's colour, a shirt tucked in).
- The small-penis rule: add an invented, unflattering detail that no one would claim, so no one claims the character. A narrator can even say the rule aloud as a running gag. Use the gag only where the book's voice can carry it.
- Never give a recognisable person an invented crime, illness, sexual act or scandal. Dark invented material goes only onto characters masked beyond recognition.
- A real public figure is never a character: they become a fictional person with their own name and history, or appear only as an object.

## 5. Test before handing over
Make a two-column table: each fact in the author's brief, and what happened to it in the character (unchanged, changed, moved or dropped). Anything unchanged other than behaviour and voice is a risk: change it, or tell the author why it stays. Then ask: could someone who knows this person pick them out from the page? If maybe, mask harder.

## 6. What gets recorded
- The book's Map and index doc record only the fictional versions, in the novel's own terms: who carries what. No real names, no private details, no "based on" lines.
- Nothing about the real people goes into memory files or doc comments.
- The brief and the sorting table stay in the chat and in scratch files that are never sent, zipped or published.
- The same rules hold in any video or illustration: no campus, building or plate that points back to the original, and faces come from the cast sheet, never from a photo.

Tell the author in a few lines who went where, what was moved and what was invented.
